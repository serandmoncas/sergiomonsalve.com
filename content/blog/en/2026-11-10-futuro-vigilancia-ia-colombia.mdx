In [part one](/en/blog/2026-11-06-vigilancia-virtual-ia-colombia) the question was whether virtual guarding saves money. The answer was yes, but it doesn't show up: AI detects and verifies, and someone still has to physically arrive. That research looked at the present, from the side of whoever buys security.

This second part looks ahead and changes its reader. It is written for people who run a security company, or who influence its decisions: managers, boards, partners, advisors. The question is different: **how will this business evolve with AI and new technologies, and which decisions are worth making now?**

I speak from two places at once. I research independently, with no contract with any vendor or guarding company, and I also build technology for this sector. I try to keep the second from contaminating the first: where a number comes from a vendor, I say so, and where it is our own estimate, I say that too.

**How I did it.** As before, I worked with Claude Code coordinating four parallel lines of research: the sector's international trajectory, how traditional companies have transformed, what will drive Colombia between 2026 and 2030, and new business models with their numbers. Claude wrote the draft from the report and I edited it. This is not legal or financial advice. Amounts are in Colombian pesos; COP 1 million is roughly USD 314.

## The thesis in one sentence

Over the next five years AI won't push the security guard out of the business. It will push out the company that only sells guard hours.

The rest of this post is the evidence for that sentence and what follows from it.

## What already happened abroad

The international trajectory disproves both extreme narratives. Guarding isn't collapsing, and technology isn't moving slowly where it matters, which is profit.

**Securitas** is the best-documented case. In 2025 its technology and solutions line was 34% of sales, with an operating margin of **11.5%**, against **6.0%** for guarding: half the operating profit from a third of the sales ([Securitas, 2025 report](https://www.securitas.com/globalassets/com/files/interim-reports/2025/q4/q42025_eng_final.pdf)). Getting there was expensive. The STANLEY Security acquisition cost USD 3.2 billion in cash, and even so the mix only rose about six points between 2022 and 2025 ([BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/securitas-buys-stanley-black-decker-unit-for-3-2-billion-1.1692713)). The long-run pace is one or two points per year.

The least-quoted lesson from Securitas is where the margin improvement came from. A large part came from **exiting unprofitable guarding contracts**, not only from selling technology. In Ibero-America, its segment most similar to Colombia, technology is already 37% of sales, and the company credits "active portfolio management" of guarding for the improvement. Pricing discipline and the technology shift go together.

**Prosegur** shows the limit of the opposite path. Its guarding business billed €2.6 billion in 2025 with a margin of just **3.47%**, despite its "hybrid security" messaging ([Prosegur, 2025 results](https://www.prosegur.com/dam//Prosegur/CORP/pdf/accionistas_inversores/informes-trimestrales-esp/2025/DIS---PCS_Resultados-FY25--espa-ol---1-.pdf)). The recurring value lives in a separate unit, alarms, with over a million customers and much higher service margins. That 3.47% looks a lot like the 3–4% of a Colombian guarding company: global scale did not turn tech-enabled guarding into a technology business.

Where the gap is huge is in how much each unit of sales is worth:

| Reference | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Securitas 2025 | Technology: 11.5% operating margin. Guarding: 6.0% |
| Prosegur Security 2025 | Guarding with hybrid messaging: 3.47% margin |
| Verisure, 2025 IPO | Enterprise value around €13.9 billion, EBITDA margin near 45% |
| ADT, sale of its commercial business | 11.2 times EBITDA |
| Typical Colombian guarding company | 3–4% margin (Fedeseguridad) |

A peso of guarding sales at a 3% margin is worth a fraction of a peso of monitoring at a 45% margin, even at the same multiple. That is the underlying financial reason to shift the mix.

The failures teach too, and they don't come from shifting to technology but from how the shift was financed. Monitronics, owner of Brinks Home, went bankrupt twice (2019 and 2023) after funding customer acquisition with debt while its attrition rose from 11% to 16% ([CEPro](https://www.cepro.com/news/brinks-home-security-parent-monitronics-files-bankruptcy/119213/)). And patrol robots failed as a standalone business: Knightscope operates with a going-concern doubt from its auditor and is growing by buying a human guarding company.

## The Colombian clock: July 2027

In Colombia the driver of change isn't technology, it's cost. A Supervigilancia resolution from August 2025 set steps in the minimum rate, tied to the surcharges in the labor reform, up to a final step of **10.38 minimum monthly wages (SMLMV) on July 1, 2027** ([Valora Analitik](https://www.valoraanalitik.com/anuncian-cambios-en-tarifas-de-vigilancia-asi-quedaron-establecidos-los-precios/)).

| Period | Factor (SMLMV) | Base rate, 24h unarmed post, before admin fee |
|---|---:|---:|
| January–June 2025 | ≈8.8 | ≈COP 12.5 million |
| From July 15, 2026 | 10.21 | COP 17.9 million |
| July–December 2027, if the minimum wage rises 7% / 9% / 12% | 10.38 | ≈COP 19.5 / 19.8 / 20.4 million |

*The 2027 figures are our own calculation: factor times an assumed minimum wage.*

With the admin fee, an unarmed post would cost about COP 21–22 million a month in the second half of 2027: **55% to 62% more than at the start of 2025**. And that lands on a sector that, according to Fedeseguridad, operates on 3% to 4% margins ([La República](https://www.larepublica.co/empresas/entrevista-con-raquel-garavito-chapaval-presidente-de-fedeseguridad-hablo-sobre-el-tamano-de-la-fuerza-laboral-del-sector-4374806)).

Two things seem key for a board:

1. **The number that moves the scenario most will be known within weeks.** For the 2027 minimum wage, unions are asking for 38.5% and business groups propose 8–9% ([La República](https://www.larepublica.co/economia/negociacion-del-minimo-para-2027-tiene-propuestas-desde-8-hasta-38-5-4517014)). The decision comes in December.
2. **The shock has an end date.** After July 2027 there are no more legal steps and cost only grows with the minimum wage. That makes 2026–2027 the window when clients review contracts most urgently, and 2028 onward a competition on productivity.

One regulatory variable could work against technology. A June 2026 draft decree would create a lower **differential rate** for households, schools, micro-businesses and small towns ([El Espectador](https://www.elespectador.com/economia/ya-hay-proyecto-de-decreto-para-bajar-tarifas-de-vigilancia-privada-en-colombia-noticias-hoy/)). It would cut the price of a post by about 15% exactly where replacement demand is highest. As of November I found no confirmation that it had been issued.

On the supply side, the bottleneck is the regulator. Supervigilancia has 94 staff and about 2,000 backlogged filings, with accreditations that can take years ([Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/negocios/empresas/retrasos-en-supervigilancia-frenan-operaciones-de-seguridad-privada-y-ponen-en-riesgo-empleos-493820)). For anyone who already holds a license, that backlog is a barrier to entry that protects them from new remote-monitoring competitors. It's an asset that doesn't show up on the balance sheet.

An important nuance about the Colombian client: they don't want to pay for technology *on top of* the post, but they will buy it *instead of* part of the post ([El Colombiano](https://www.elcolombiano.com/negocios/empresas/empresas-de-vigilancia-sin-tecnologia-desaparecen-BE35574423)). The winning offer here is framed as savings on the current bill, not as added value.

## What AI actually changes

Almost every monitoring-center software vendor launched "AI agents" in 2025–2026: they triage alarms, close the ones that don't need dispatch, describe what they see and talk to intruders over a speaker. Ambient, for example, says one customer automatically closed 94% of 240,000 alarms ([SecurityInfoWatch](https://www.securityinfowatch.com/ai/article/55379016/the-machine-that-watches-everything)). That's a vendor figure, and the same article acknowledges that human verification "is the business." I found no independent evidence of a monitoring center running without a person in the loop.

So the economic conclusion is concrete: **AI doesn't eliminate the operator, it multiplies what the operator produces.** In the model from part one, at COP 5 million per site, going from 10 to 40 sites per operator takes gross margin from 34% to 61%. That rewards scale and compresses monitoring prices. The metric that matters stops being how many cameras you have and becomes **what each verified event costs you**.

Because these features arrive inside the monitoring software already sold in the region, Colombia's technology lag may be short. The organizational lag, less so. This is my estimate, with no formal measurement behind it:

| Technology | Estimated lag in Colombia | What it means for the business |
|---|---|---|
| Remote concierge and remote guarding for residences | 3–5 years behind Brazil | Sustained growth through 2031, with labor disputes |
| Cameras with on-device AI | 1–2 years | Comes standard; differentiates no one |
| AI agents in the monitoring center | 1–3 years | The difference is organizational, not technical |
| Pure cloud video | 3–5 years | Hybrid edge-plus-cloud wins on bandwidth |
| Drones at private sites | 3–5 years | First at large industrial sites |
| Patrol robots | Not before 2030 | Failed as a business abroad |

**Brazil is the mirror.** It already has more than 14,000 condominiums with remote concierge, growing between 14% and 24% a year ([SíndicoNet](https://www.sindiconet.com.br/Informese/26778/Portaria-Virtual/Portaria-Remota-x-Presencial-tecnologia-custo-e-seguranca-em-jogo)). The path wasn't linear: a law banned it in the Federal District (struck down by the Supreme Court in May 2026), collective agreements penalize replacing doormen, and some condominiums went back to hybrid models. My reading for Colombia is that adoption will be real and sustained, but with litigation, union pressure and partial reversals. Hybrid by time slot will be the stable form in many residential complexes.

**Object AI advances; identity AI stalls.** Detecting intrusion, loitering, vehicles or weapons is advancing with no meaningful regulatory brake. Identifying people is going the other way. In the United States, 149 cities and counties canceled or rejected Flock's license plate cameras between January and August 2026, not because of the technology but because of how authorities used the data ([Jalopnik](https://www.jalopnik.com/2276190/number-of-cities-dropped-flock-cameras-quietly-exploding/)). In Colombia, the data protection authority (SIC) has already ordered a residential complex to delete facial data and offer entry by card or code. My recommendation for any 2027–2031 offering: build it on object and event AI, and treat facial recognition as an option with legal risk, not as the product's foundation.

## Antioquia goes against the trend

Nationally, extortion peaked in 2024 and fell slightly in 2025. **Medellín, however, rose to 980 cases in 2025** and was the country's second city ([El Colombiano](https://www.elcolombiano.com/inicio/medellin-segunda-ciudad-extorsiones-colombia-cifras-EL42588914)). The city's report at week 32 of 2026 shows extortion up 16% and street theft up 8% ([Alcaldía de Medellín](https://www.medellin.gov.co/es/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Balance-delictivo-y-operativo-semana-32-2026.pdf)). That profile favors access control, verification and response in residential complexes and small businesses.

Public camera networks are opening up to private ones. Medellín's SIES-M has about 3,230 cameras and a modernization contract that contemplates integrating third-party systems, and in Antioquia about 2,100 private cameras are reportedly already connected to the departmental hub ([Telemedellín](https://telemedellin.tv/?p=181961)). "Connected to SIES-M" can be a selling point.

And there's a dated risk. XM, the grid operator, projects a firm-energy deficit, and there's a high probability of a strong El Niño with a critical phase between December 2026 and April 2027 ([Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/energia/xm-advierte-riesgo-de-racionamiento-por-deficit-de-energia-frente-a-la-demanda-y-retrasos-en-nuevos-proyectos-497564)). Rationing would do two things at once: raise demand for security and stress-test any remote system without batteries, a generator and a backup cellular link.

## Eight opportunities, from most cautious to most ambitious

The ranking is our own judgment, based on available evidence and the capital each one requires. None has Colombian retention or margin data; the ranges come from vendors or foreign comparables.

| Opportunity | Client | How it makes money | Capital | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid by time slot with own response | Complexes, retail, industry with current posts | Fewer posts + monitoring center + motorcycle response | Low–medium | Rejection by owners' assembly; liability if response fails |
| Night monitoring of empty sites | Warehouses, construction, yards, parking | Monthly fee or solar tower rental | Medium | Equipment theft; arrival times |
| Recurring revenue unit | Homes, shops, SMEs | Fee with loaned equipment, 24–36 months | Medium–high | Customer attrition and acquisition cost |
| Residential and neighborhood camera networks | Complexes, street-front retail | Multi-year subscription with loaned equipment | High | Privacy, reputation, patient capital |
| Integration with SIES-M, C4 and hubs | Private clients connecting cameras | Contract add-on | Low | Political cycle; use of the data |
| Reselling AI loss prevention | Shops and restaurants | Per-store license + response | Low | Third-party product; thin margin |
| Power and link continuity | Every monitored site | Installation + maintenance | Low | Opportunistic sale if there's no rationing |
| Operator school and retraining | The company itself and the sector | Courses; lower turnover | Low | Small as a business on its own |

The first four have the best balance of evidence and risk. A few notes on them:

**Hybrid with response is the mandatory defensive move.** It's what Securitas and Prosegur already do, and Securitas counts it inside its guarding line, not as technology. For a Colombian guarding company it's how you avoid losing the contract when the owners' assembly decides to cut. Yes, it cannibalizes your own post, but if you don't, someone else will. What separates a serious hybrid from an improvised one is physical response: a motorcycle unit with arrival times measured per address.

**Sites that are empty at night capture the savings best.** Anyone detected after hours is an alarm, the operator gets few events, there's no assembly to convince and almost no personal data at stake. In the United States, monitored solar towers rent for USD 1,000 to 3,500 a month, according to vendors. In Colombia I found no published prices for this service, which is both an information gap and a sign of an underserved market.

**The recurring revenue unit requires a company within the company.** It's the model with the best known numbers: at Verisure, each customer pays about €46 a month, costs €1,486 to acquire and churns at 7.4% a year ([Verisure](https://www.verisure.com/system/files/private_pdf/2025-11/verisure-q3-2025-interim-report.pdf)). But it's won with mass sales, marketing, collections and retention, capabilities a B2B guarding company usually lacks. That's why Prosegur runs it separately, and why it makes sense to do the same or partner with a bank or a telecom operator.

**Neighborhood networks are the highest-value and highest-patience bet.** The most useful Latin American comparable is Gabriel, in Brazil: street-facing cameras on buildings and shops, loaned equipment, four-year contracts, about 22,000 cameras. It reached breakeven **after six years** ([Startups.com.br](https://startups.com.br/negocios/exclusivo-gabriel-atinge-breakeven-e-amplia-presenca-para-45-cidades/)). That's the realistic benchmark: patient capital, not hypergrowth. And it demands flawless data handling before the SIC, because the Flock case shows legitimacy is lost through how data is used, not through the camera.

Of the other four, the one that can't wait is **power continuity**: batteries, UPS, generator and backup cellular link before the critical dry season. It's the easiest to sell in the next six months, and it also protects the reputation of your own remote service.

## A board's three decisions

For a board, the question is no longer whether to invest in technology. It's these three.

**1. Where to stand relative to platforms.** Globally, the dominant model is one where the platform charges a monthly fee per account and the local company brands, sells, installs and responds. There are three possible positions:

- **Platform partner:** fast and capital-light, but you give up the software margin, and the vendor can go straight to the client.
- **Owner of the client and the monitoring center, on a platform open to multiple manufacturers:** requires more integration, but reduces dependence.
- **Own platform:** the highest value, and the most capital and product team.

For most mid-sized guarding companies, the second seems the sensible position to me. Lock-in isn't in the cameras but in cloud licenses, historical data and long contracts. It's worth negotiating video portability, per-camera price caps and the right to use standard (ONVIF) equipment from other manufacturers.

**2. Discipline in guarding.** The Securitas case suggests margin is won as much by pruning contracts that don't cover their real cost as by selling technology. With 3–4% margins, a guarding company can't fund a technology shift with contracts that lose money. And there are documented traps abroad with local equivalents: subsidizing equipment without measuring attrition, borrowing to buy monitoring portfolios, and promising "total security," which turns an obligation of means into a commitment to results.

**3. Grow, partner or sell.** Allied Universal, the sector's largest employer worldwide, bought the Colombian company Celar Security / Soltes Technology in 2025, described as a guarding and technology firm ([GlobeNewswire](https://rss.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/11/18/3190001/0/en/Allied-Universal-Details-Recent-Merger-and-Acquisition-Activities-Targeting-Strategic-Growth.html)). It's the clearest signal that consolidators value that combination. With thin margins, costs up about 30% in one year and a slow regulator, a wave of consolidation among small companies between 2026 and 2028 seems plausible to me, though I found no data confirming it. For a mid-sized guarding company, building a technology line with measurable recurring revenue raises its value, even with no intention to sell.

### The calendar

| Date | Event | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| December 2026 | 2027 minimum wage and rate circular | Sets the price technology competes against |
| December 2026 to April 2027 | Critical El Niño phase | Sell power backup; test your own monitoring center |
| No date | Differential rate decree | If issued, reduces the savings from replacement in homes and SMEs |
| June 20, 2027 | End of the legislative session | Without an AI law, the SIC remains the de facto regulator |
| July 1, 2027 | Last rate step | The cost shock ends; productivity competition begins |
| 2027–2029 | AI agents in the monitoring center | Sites per operator set the price of monitoring |

### Three indicators to watch every quarter

Instead of market forecasts, I suggest tracking three of your own numbers:

1. What share of revenue comes from technology and monitoring.
2. How many sites each operator covers and what each verified event costs.
3. How long response actually takes to arrive, in the worst 10% of cases, by zone.

No Colombian company publishes these figures. The one that measures them and uses them to sell will have an argument no one can match with brochures.

## What I see from the builder's side

I'm building GuardIA, which I wrote about in [part one](/en/blog/2026-11-06-vigilancia-virtual-ia-colombia): a self-hosted home video surveillance system with ESP32 cameras and local detection. It's a small personal project and isn't meant to compete with anyone mentioned here. But building it taught me something this research confirmed: AI that detects people or vehicles is already off the shelf. Anyone can get it, even with low-cost components.

That's why I believe the advantage won't be in the AI. It will be in what can't be imported: a clean license in a sector under review, motorcycle units that arrive on time in every neighborhood, relationships with property managers and assemblies, trained operators and data handling that stands up to the SIC. A company that sees itself as running a chain of detect, verify and arrive, rather than as a staffing provider, has in AI a tool to double its margin. One that doesn't has in it the competitor that will take its contract.

## Limits of this research

- Almost all performance figures come from the companies themselves or from vendors.
- There's no public data on the technology shift of Colombian guarding companies, or on customer retention or sites per operator in Latin America.
- The 2027 rate projections, the lag map and the margin model are our own estimates.
- The differential rate decree and the 2027 minimum wage had not been set when this post was published.
- This is not legal or financial advice. Before deciding, check with a lawyer who knows the sector and with your own company's data.
